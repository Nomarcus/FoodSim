How it works

Two teams, blue and orange, play by volleyball rules: the server hits from behind the end line, each side gets at most three touches, the same player may not touch twice in a row, the ball must cross between the antennas, and a ball that lands outside the lines or touches a wall or the ceiling is out. Rally scoring, sets to 25 (win by 2); the team that wins a rally serves, and rotates when it wins the serve back.

How it learns: each brain plays 12 rallies against the coach, a coded orange team with a skill level from 1 to 8. Fitness is mostly points won. The generation's best brain is then checked on 24 fresh rallies; when it wins 70% of them, the coach moves up a level. The chart shows that check and every level-up.

On each touch the brain decides whether to pass (code then lobs it to the best-placed teammate: a forearm bump low, a two-hand set high) or to attack – a one-arm swing on a high ball, aimed by the brain. Passes that are played on, and balls sent over after two or three touches by different players, earn extra.

The evolved brain decides six things per player, 60 times a second: whether to spike (code then times the jump), the hit angle, the sideways aim, the power, pass or attack, and whether to throw a backflip in the air. The server's brain also picks the serve's angle, power and direction. Ordinary code runs each player to the ball (if it is theirs to take) or back to their spot. It sees the ball, where the ball will come down, its teammate and the net, all mirrored so every player thinks it plays from the left.

Ordinary code animates the body: spine, head, three-part arms (upper arm, forearm, hand) and three-part legs (thigh, shin, foot) are posed with inverse kinematics so the hands reach for the ball. Hands and forearms strike, shins and feet kick (upside down in a flip that is a bicycle kick), and every other part only deflects the ball. Each part is drawn exactly as wide as its collision shape – tick “Show hitboxes” to see them.

Every genome in a generation receives the same serves, so their scores are directly comparable. Training rallies stop after 30 s and then count for neither side; the match on show has no time limit and is played to the end.

The court is 3D: players move forwards, backwards and sideways, each has a home spot so the team covers the whole half, and a ball landing outside the lines is out. The 2D view is a side-on look at the same court. Drag the 3D view to orbit, scroll to zoom.
